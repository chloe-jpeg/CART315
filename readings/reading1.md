Pippin’s second chapter of his book focuses on his PONGS variations, explaining some of them, the thought process behind the idea as well as what he finds remarkable in each iteration. 
What I really liked about the reading, other than the fact that it was wildly entertaining, was that each modification was more interesting than the last. Pippin went to great lengths to make them as intricate as possible, and more importantly, didn’t stop at just the game. Like the Vienna game, nothing about the actual gameplay has changed, but the rules outside of it have. It stretches the limit over what can be called a good modification, how original it makes the game. But if we just based ourselves on how “good” a modification has to be to be part of the challenge, then could we say that the ghost game is a good modification? There is nothing that the players can do to participate in the game, but it is undeniably an interesting level in any case. 
I especially liked the quote “The rules, which often seem set in stone, suddenly become material for playful exploration through design, and they are free to ask, What if?” Pippin Bar, p. 26. The difference between a board game and a video game is that even after the official release, video games are still subject to change and updates. Even if the final version is offered, the fact that you still have the opportunity of working on it leaves a world of possibilities.
