For this second reading, I found it interesting that the interview was about the design of something, often an object and how the brand or particular item or software became important to people, how they were used and why they were used. 
In the interview, Donald A. Schön talks about why something created becomes popular or generates a lot of use. But what I found particularly engaging in the text was the passage about listening to feedback, to have a conversation with the materials. The biggest example used is the Scotch tape story, and what makes it interesting is how the company inventing it only had a small vision of what it could be used for. But by seeing how their customers used it, they realized that what a small pool of people had decided didn't mean that it could be its only use. People are creative and the company understood that, adapted to their needs and now have a pretty big monopoly on the whole idea, and all the small ones that got added. Later in the interview, Schön also talks about how the same thing happened to the two programs used in this MIT course, how the students worked with them but not in the way intended by the designer. It’s only by adapting to the audience that a product can become essential. 
I can see how this reading, while seeming disconnected to the topic of games, can be used in our class. We are lucky to work with a medium that lets us update it as we see fit. As mentioned in the end of Pippin’s second chapter, big games are often not 100% finished when published, and even if they are, bugs and unforeseen mechanics are always a part of them. It’s by having the public interact with the product and listening to feedback that change can be made. Updates to the game to maximize the enjoyment of the player can only happen if we actually listen to what the player wants. In that case we are in a conversation with the user, as said by Schön.
